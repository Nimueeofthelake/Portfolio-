Historical Ideas

One of the earliest conceptions of artificial intelligence came from Warren McCulloch and Walter Pitts, who proposed a mathematical model that explored whether artificial systems could imitate the behavior of biological neurons. However, the foundations of modern AI are often traced back to Alan Turing. Turing explored whether humans interacting with machines could distinguish between communicating with an AI and communicating with another human. If a machine could convince a human that they were interacting with another person, it would demonstrate a form of human-like intelligence. What this ultimately brought about was a much larger question: Could human intelligence be simulated by a machine?

In both the early stages of AI development and the modern era, the original idea of "Can AI mimic human thought?" has continued to become increasingly relevant. However, ideas such as these are nothing new. In mythology, similar concepts can be seen in the Greek myth of Talos, who was created by Hephaestus to protect the island of Crete. The idea of an artificial creation performing tasks or even coming to life is not new; rather, the form it has taken has drastically changed as technology has improved.

Milestones

One of the first major milestones in early AI development was the creation of the Logic Theorist and General Problem Solver, which used mathematical logic, established rules, and patterns to solve specific problems. In 1966, ELIZA introduced a new approach by using symbolic reasoning and pattern matching to interact with users. ELIZA was one of the world's first chatbots, designed to simulate a psychotherapist through keyword-based conversational patterns. The basic premise of how ELIZA worked was that its scanner analyzed user input for keywords, compared them to decomposition rules, assembled a response using a template, and produced an output that gave the impression of conversational awareness. ELIZA laid the groundwork for future chatbots and advancements in natural language processing.

As the industry grew and AI became more publicly accessible, its popularity increased during the 2000s. The growth of the internet provided access to large-scale datasets containing images, text, and other learning materials that data-driven models needed to develop. From this foundation emerged AlexNet, which used a deep neural network to successfully identify objects across millions of labeled images. AlexNet was a foundational breakthrough because it demonstrated the potential of deep learning and significantly advanced image recognition. Its success helped establish deep neural networks as a major approach in modern AI research. This development made increasingly complex tasks possible, including image recognition, natural language processing, and other computational applications. AlexNet and the broader development of deep learning helped open the door to the rapid expansion of AI.

This growth in AI eventually led to the development of Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs), which allowed AI to generate realistic images, as well as significant improvements in large language models. Together, these contributions have helped lead to the current AI boom, characterized by advanced reasoning models and AI agents capable of making progress in mathematics, coding, and complex problem-solving. These developments have contributed to the rise of agentic AI, in which systems can increasingly plan and perform tasks independently.

Limitations

Limitations within the field of AI have often stemmed from a lack of research, investment, and external support. An example of this can be seen in the 1970s, a period known as the "AI winter," when progress failed to meet expectations and funding declined. It was not until the 1980s that interest in AI was renewed and research received additional funding. What this reveals about the industry is that the development of AI, like that of many emerging technologies, is fueled by two major factors: funding and public interest. One of the main reasons AI experienced a decline during the 1970s was that researchers and developers had overpromised what the technology could achieve, causing investors to lose confidence and interest. AI has experienced a major resurgence in recent years because of the growing interest companies have in using it to automate tasks and improve operational efficiency. Investors are more willing to provide funding when a technology demonstrates promising results, and in the current era of AI, its capabilities are advancing rapidly.
